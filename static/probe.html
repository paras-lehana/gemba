Your phone becomes a machine sensor

Most machines in small factories are 10 to 25 years old and have no network port. A phone held against the machine can listen (microphone) and feel the shaking (motion sensor). It works out three numbers four times a second and sends only those numbers to Gemba, never the sound itself.

VMC-202 is a vertical machining centre in the fictional Pune plant. Your phone's readings are real; the machine is simulated.

How to read these numbers
  1. Main pitch is the loudest tone. A worn spindle bearing adds a hum or whine at a steady pitch; a change of pitch is often the first sign of wear.
  2. Loudness is the average strength of the sound. Above 0.2 is loud.
  3. Shaking is how hard the phone is being shaken, without gravity. A phone on a desk reads about 0.05; above 1.8 counts as heavy shaking.
  4. One loud reading can be a knock or a door. Gemba only raises the alarm after 4 in a row (one second).
  5. Then Gemba Watch on the laptop notices the machine and starts the agents, the same way a real alarm would.
